## Bring a small test kit

Take a known-good cassette you can afford to lose, a supported blank, headphones and a line-level source with the right cables. Ask the seller's permission to record over the blank. Obtain the exact model manual and clarify which advertised repairs were actually done.

1. Inspect the unplugged deck and accessible tape path. Look for corrosion, damaged connectors, cracked rollers and heavy residue. Avoid touching screws or forcing doors.
2. Play the familiar cassette with its correct NR/EQ settings. Listen to both channels, a sustained piano/guitar note and a quiet passage. Watch for irregular travel; stop at any sign of tape being pulled out.
3. Test STOP, PAUSE, rewind and fast forward with the expendable tape. Do not force a sluggish mechanism. If auto-reverse is present, check both directions; if it has two wells, test each separately.
4. Connect the source's stereo line output to LINE IN L/R. Record a short passage on your blank at conservative level. Stop, rewind and replay: hearing the input monitor alone does not test the recorded tape.
5. Compare source and playback for balance, treble and stable pitch. Test the NR mode you actually plan to use, not just whether its lamp lights.

## Use the findings in the decision

A [quiet channel](../quiet-channel/), consistently wrong [speed](../playback-speed-problems/) or a transport that eats tape warrants investigation or a repair budget. “Needs a belt” is a possible explanation, not a diagnosis established by a seller's description. A working demonstration also does not certify wow/flutter, head alignment or long-term reliability.

Ask for the service history, return conditions and any unavailable accessories you need. If recording cannot be demonstrated, treat recording ability as unverified. After purchase, establish safe transport and [cleaning](../clean-cassette-deck/) before measuring or storing correction profiles. Do not use a valuable original cassette as the first home test.
