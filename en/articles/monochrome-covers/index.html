J-cards & printing

Black-and-White Cassette Covers That Work on a Home Printer

A good black-and-white cover uses differences in lightness, not colour alone.

MixtapeForge editorial teamUpdated November 1, 20262 min read
In this guide
  1. Build a printable version
  2. Save ink without losing information
← Back to articles
Quick answer

A good black-and-white cover uses differences in lightness, not colour alone. Lavender and cyan can become similar grey even though they look distinct on screen.

Build a printable version

Use a monochrome printer or grayscale mode, plain paper and the actual case.

  1. In MixtapeForge’s Cover screen, start with B/W archive for an economical layout or B&W fanzines for a denser graphic treatment. Set paper light and text dark.
  2. Enable the grayscale-print checkbox. The conversion also applies to the PDF and SVG, so check the new preview rather than assuming the colours remain distinguishable.
  3. Inspect text against every background, especially the spine and small side labels. Keep a distinction in brightness when changing custom accents.
  4. Print one copy at 100%. Read it through the case. If neighbouring shapes merge, change their brightness or simplify the motif; turning up printer darkness will not separate equal greys.

Save ink without losing information

Large black rectangles use more toner than a white background with black lettering. Printer draft mode can also make thin strokes weak: compare a normal-quality proof before blaming the font. The app’s contrast warning is a useful screen check, not a guarantee of ink, paper and plastic behaviour.

Use the typography checks for small text and paper testing for smearing or fold cracks. A colour cover that happens to print grey is not necessarily a finished monochrome design.

Accept the proof when: title, spine and every song remain readable, important shapes stay separate, and no information relies only on colour.
